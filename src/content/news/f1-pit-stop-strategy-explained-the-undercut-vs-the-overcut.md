Follow a car closely enough at the Hungaroring, and you will inevitably ruin your own race.

The turbulent, dirty air throwing off the back of the leading car overheats your front tyres, strips away your aerodynamic downforce, and turns your steering wheel into a suggestion rather than a command. You can be half a second faster in pure pace, but on a tight circuit, overtaking on the asphalt is nearly impossible.

So, you beat them in the pit lane. Because pit stop tactics require a completely different mindset than pure tyre management, they demand their own tactical breakdown. When drivers cannot pass on track, race engineers deploy two distinct strategic weapons to leapfrog their rivals: the undercut and the overcut.

## The Undercut: Striking First

The undercut is the most aggressive strategic move in modern Formula 1. It relies entirely on the massive grip advantage of brand-new Pirelli rubber.

Think of it like taking a clear backroad to bypass a traffic jam while the car ahead stays on a congested highway. You take a brief detour into the pits, but you gain access to a much higher speed limit, eventually merging back in front.

Imagine Lando Norris is stuck directly behind Max Verstappen at Zandvoort. Verstappen defends perfectly into Tarzan corner every lap. Norris simply cannot pass.

McLaren’s pit wall decides to pull the trigger. They box Norris a lap earlier than Verstappen’s scheduled stop. Norris sheds his worn rubber, bolts on a fresh set of Soft tyres, and rejoins the track.

Verstappen stays out for one more lap on old, degrading tyres.

This single lap is the entire battle. Norris uses his fresh rubber to set a blistering "out-lap," instantly finding two or three seconds of pace that Verstappen cannot match on dying tyres. When Red Bull brings Verstappen into the pits on the very next lap, the math catches up to them. Verstappen exits the pit lane just as Norris flies past on the main straight at 200 mph.

McLaren stole the position without ever making an on-track pass.

### The Enemies of the Undercut

* **Traffic:** If you pit early and rejoin the track behind a midfield scrap, your fresh tyre advantage is instantly neutralised. You are stuck in dirty air again.
* **Slow Pit Stops:** A standard F1 pit stop takes roughly 2.2 seconds. If a wheel gun jams and the stop takes 4.5 seconds, the undercut window slams shut instantly.

## The Overcut: Playing the Long Game

The overcut is the exact opposite strategy. Instead of pitting early, a driver stays out on track after the car ahead decides to pit.

This tactic is rarer and much harder to execute. It works best under specific conditions, usually when tyre warm-up is a severe issue or a track has unusually low tyre degradation.

You cannot discuss extending tyre life without walking down the pit lane to the Ferrari garage. Lewis Hamilton built a legacy on the overcut.

Hamilton reads the race from the cockpit better than the supercomputers on the pit wall. He manages thermal degradation so precisely that he can keep a set of Mediums alive long after the Pirelli engineers say the cliff has arrived. When his engineer asks for two more laps to make an overcut work, Hamilton already has the grip banked to deliver them.

The overcut is the ultimate display of patience. It requires a driver to possess unnatural pace on ageing rubber while banking on their rival making a mistake on cold tyres.

## The Pit Wall Variables

Executing either strategy requires race engineers to monitor a dizzying array of live telemetry. They are not just watching their own car; they are mapping the entire circuit.

**The Pit Stop Delta:** Engineers know exactly how much time a pit stop costs at every track. At Silverstone, driving through the pit lane at the speed limit takes about 28 seconds. If you want to drop your driver into clean air for an undercut, you need to find a 28-second gap in the traffic behind you.

**Track Temperature:** A cold track heavily favours the overcut, as new tyres take multiple laps to switch on. A scorching 50°C track in the middle of summer heavily favours the undercut, as fresh rubber grips the boiling asphalt immediately.

Tires dictate the raw physical grip available, but timing dictates the trophy. When a race engineer presses the radio button and yells, "Box, box," they are not just changing tyres. They are springing a mathematical trap that takes twenty laps to set and exactly one lap to execute. Who will blink first?
