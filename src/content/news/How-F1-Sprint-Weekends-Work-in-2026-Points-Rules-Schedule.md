Walk through the pit lane on a traditional Friday afternoon, and you will see mechanics casually taking apart gearboxes while drivers study telemetry on long, heavy-fuel practice runs.

On a Sprint weekend, that relaxed Friday atmosphere is entirely gone.

By Friday afternoon, the 22 drivers are strapped in, the engines are turned up to maximum, and the first qualifying session of the weekend is already underway.

Formula 1 introduced the Sprint format to inject adrenaline into all three days of a Grand Prix weekend. In 2026, six circuits - including Shanghai, Miami, and Montreal - host this hyper-condensed schedule. Here is exactly how the modern F1 Sprint weekend works, how the championship points are allocated, and why the Friday rule changes catch so many teams out.

## The 2026 Weekend Schedule: Zero Margin for Error

A standard Grand Prix weekend gives teams three full hours of free practice to dial in their suspension setups. A Sprint weekend slashes that preparation time down to just 60 minutes.

* **Friday:** Free Practice 1 (60 mins) followed directly by Sprint Qualifying.
* **Saturday:** The 100km Sprint Race in the morning, followed by standard Grand Prix Qualifying in the afternoon.
* **Sunday:** The main event - the Grand Prix.

With Cadillac joining the grid and 22 cars fighting for track space, that single practice session on Friday morning is chaotic. If McLaren brings an aerodynamic upgrade for Lando Norris, they have exactly one hour to figure out if it works before the car is locked into a competitive session.

## Sprint Qualifying: The Shortened Shootout

Friday afternoon decides the starting grid for Saturday’s Sprint. The FIA uses a condensed, three-part knockout format specifically for this session.

* **SQ1 (12 Minutes):** All 22 cars hit the track. The six slowest are eliminated and locked into positions 17 through 22.
* **SQ2 (10 Minutes):** The remaining 16 cars fight for survival. Another six drop out, taking positions 11 through 16.
* **SQ3 (8 Minutes):** The top 10 battle for pole position for the Sprint. Eight minutes is barely enough time to complete an out-lap, a flying lap, and an in-lap.

### The Mandatory Tyre Rule

You cannot bolt on your fastest rubber whenever you want. The FIA strictly mandates which tyres drivers must use during Sprint Qualifying.

During SQ1 and SQ2, every driver must run a new set of Medium (yellow-banded) tyres. In SQ3, they switch to the Soft (red-banded) compound. Drivers have to completely recalibrate their braking points for that final eight-minute shootout because the Soft tyre offers vastly more physical grip than the Medium.

## The 100-Kilometre Dash

Saturday morning is pure, uninterrupted racing. The Sprint covers 100 kilometres - roughly one-third of a standard Grand Prix distance. At a massive circuit like Spa-Francorchamps, that equates to just 15 laps.

There are no mandatory pit stops. Teams bolt on a set of Medium or Soft tyres and simply drive flat-out for 30 minutes. It completely removes the strategic chess match of undercuts and tyre management that dominates Sunday.

Points are awarded to the top eight finishers, and they count directly towards both the Drivers' and Constructors' World Championships:

* **1st Place:** 8 points
* **2nd Place:** 7 points
* **3rd Place:** 6 points
* **4th Place:** 5 points
* **5th Place:** 4 points
* **6th Place:** 3 points
* **7th Place:** 2 points
* **8th Place:** 1 point

## The Double Parc Fermé Window

This is the most critical rule change for the modern Sprint era.

Historically, once a car entered qualifying on Friday, its mechanical setup was locked under Parc Fermé rules for the entire weekend. If you chose the wrong ride height, you were stuck with it on Sunday.

The 2026 rules use a split Parc Fermé window.

When Mercedes sends Kimi Antonelli out for Sprint Qualifying on Friday, they lock the proverbial bonnet. They cannot change the suspension geometry or ride height through the Sprint Race.

However, as soon as the chequered flag drops on the Saturday Sprint, Parc Fermé opens back up. Engineers have a golden window of a few hours to analyse the telemetry, tear the car down, and completely change the setup before the standard Grand Prix Qualifying begins on Saturday afternoon.

It acts as a real-world test session. If Red Bull realises their car is shredding its rear tyres during the Sprint, they can physically soften the rear suspension before Sunday.
